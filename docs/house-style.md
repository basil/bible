# House style: checklists

Unpublished. These are the rules a change is checked against during review; the reasons for them are given to the reader in [the introduction](../content/introduction.sfm), and the decisions in `edition/*.json` define the exact scope and exceptions of every change. The introduction supplies context, not additional checklist requirements. The examples here authorize no further substitutions. [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md) explains how the build carries the decisions out, [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md) how each kind is reviewed, [the New Testament guide](new-testament.md) the reconciliation's own house style, and [the numbering guide](numbering.md) the detailed numbering rule and witness exceptions.

## Texts, books, and titles

- Prepare Brenton from eBible's transcription, including its corrections, and the New Testament from eBible's Cambridge Paragraph Bible of 1873. The Updated Brenton supplies line boundaries alone. Daniel remains Theodotion's version throughout. Source versions and notices belong in [sources/README.md](../sources/README.md).
- Follow the Church of Greece's Old Testament order, without a separate Apocrypha section, except that the Prayer of Manasses follows 2 Chronicles and 4 Maccabees follows 3 Maccabees. Keep the English New Testament order, Hebrews after Philemon and then the Catholic Epistles. The manifest defines the books and order; count Daniel with its additions as one book, and the Epistle of Jeremias and Prayer of Manasses separately: 51 Old Testament books, 78 in all.
- Use the manifest's Orthodox book names and full titles in the Cambridge Paragraph Bible's style, extending that style to books it lacks. Numbered books put the number first; the four books of Kingdoms include their familiar English equivalents in their full titles. Write New Testament "S." as "Saint" and call the letters of James, Peter, John, and Jude Catholic Epistles. Do not derive new name substitutions from a book heading.
- Take running-head names and citation abbreviations from the registries. The Psalter's running head names one "Psalm"; Susanna and Bel and the Dragon use their own headings in running heads and bookmarks. The contents gives each unit's title alone, its folio in the inner margin; preliminaries are italic, and each testament's row is small capitals without a folio.
- Split eBible's Ezra–Nehemiah into 2 Esdras and Nehemias. Keep Susanna before Daniel 1, Bel and the Dragon after Daniel 12, the Prayer and Song within Daniel 3, the additions to Esther lettered within the chapters they follow, and Psalm 151 at the end of the Psalter. Sirach's Prologue is an unnumbered introduction; the Epistle of Jeremias has an unnumbered superscription.

## Conforming the New Testament

- RP2026's main Greek text is the target; other editions and witnesses supply evidence, never a replacement target. Compare it with Scrivener's reconstruction of the Received Text; account for every Greek difference and validate each decision against the pinned evidence.
- Judge translatability at the King James Version's level: meaning, reference, number, tense, mood, voice, emphasis, or grammatical relation. Keep the English where it already carries the difference or cannot show it. An article, particle, inflection, or word order alone does not require a change; do not make a passage more literal than its neighbours.
- Check Pierpont's instructions against RP2026 rather than applying them unchanged. Start from Pierpont's instruction where one fits the Greek and house style; where none executes or its wording fails, prefer a fitting King James parallel, then Pierpont, the 1881 Revised Version, Boyd's 2021 Byzantine ASV, Boyd's Text-Critical English New Testament, Far Above All, and finally the editor's own wording. The Revised Version's automatic witness requires Westcott–Hort to agree with the Byzantine reading at the unit; hand borrowings require their own evidence and decision, as [the New Testament guide](new-testament.md) explains.
- A reading that follows a King James parallel (`kjv-parallel` in `wording`) gives its `kjv` evidence, a `ref` and a `quote` and nothing else, and only such a reading gives it: the quote is found once in the pinned verse, in whole words, with supplied words bracketed, and is not words any decision edits.
- Judge the whole sentence: retain King James diction, difficult readings, and ambiguity; change dependent words and punctuation only where the construction requires it. Do not harmonize, paraphrase to explain, or splice incompatible instructions. Mark necessary supplied English and remove its supplied marking where RP2026 now has the Greek; remove supplied words that served only a construction now omitted.
- Omit Luke 17:36, Acts 8:37, 15:34, and 24:7, with the related clauses in Acts 24:6 and 8; leave their verse numbers unused. Set the doxology at Romans 14:24–26, ending Romans at 16:24; exchange Matthew 23:13–14 under the numbers of their places. Both verses of 1 John 5:7–8 remain with their Byzantine contents.
- A correction where the Greek is shared must render that shared Greek, touch no Greek difference, and document its English source. Prefer the translators' own margin where it offers the better rendering; a difference of English style alone changes nothing. Give the former English in an "or," note without a witness. Verify that restoring the notes reconstructs the pinned King James wording, capitals and stops included.
- Keep the fourteen epistles' subscriptions apart from scripture, preserving their wording rather than conforming them to the Byzantine manuscripts; apply the edition's name revisions. Supply the four Gospel publication subscriptions from Boyd’s TCENT, without his introductory note wording, in the same form after each Gospel’s final verse; their dates are measured from Christ’s ascension. Omit Acts' closing notice.

## Numbering and placement

- Follow [the numbering guide](numbering.md) for evidence and exceptions: eBible's numbers are the base, usually Rahlfs's; the Church of Greece's replace them where an eligible second witness agrees. Brenton's borrowed numbers have no independent vote. A number found only on the Church of Greece's website is not sufficient evidence.
- Redivide only with the required agreement of witnesses and where Brenton's English can take the boundary. A new division must begin his sentence or an independent clause, not a phrase, list item, or dependent second verb. Preserve his own division where the detailed rule protects it. Check opening words and adjacent boundaries, not labels alone.
- Leave a number unused rather than attach it to words no witness gives it to. Count psalm titles as verses, keep the Greek chapter order of Jeremias, and retain supplied Alexandrine verses under Swete's or Brenton's Appendix's numbers. Daniel's Prayer and Song run within 3:24–90, not a separate 1–67; Malachias has four chapters, ending in the Greek verse order.
- Proverbs' displaced passages stay in their Greek positions within chapter 24 under their Hebrew numbers. Print the chapter on the first verse of each passage and on the return to chapter 24; after 29:27 pass to 31:10, with the note locating the Hebrew's passages. Do not print eBible's empty chapter 30 or its remark.
- Convert references to the edition's names and numbers; the website's comparison tables use the King James names and numbers in their King James column. Keep verses absent from one Bible distinct from verses merely numbered or placed otherwise. Derive tables and quotation passages from the prepared documents and placement, not copied text.

## Readings from Codex Alexandrinus

A reading of Codex Alexandrinus that Brenton reported is printed in the text only where every item holds:

- Swete's edition supports the reading and its place in the verse, from his text or his apparatus; the decision names the volume and page and quotes the evidence. Swete's silence keeps the report a note, with `agrees` null. Where Swete disagrees with Brenton, follow Swete and document the disagreement in the decision.
- The reading is not doubtful: not over an erasure in an uncertain hand, not read "apparently". A corrected reading may be printed. Printed notes name the manuscript alone; information about its hands, correctors, erasures, and marginal corrections belongs in decision reasons and review materials.
- The reading is not an evident slip of the scribe: a name spelled against the manuscript's own spelling elsewhere (Chaph for Cham, Genesis 5:32, 6:10), a number that contradicts its context (Numbers 28:24, 2 Kingdoms 8:4, 3 Kingdoms 15:2).
- The English is Brenton's own, from the note that gave the reading, or the displaced words edited, or words supplied and declared one by one from a named public-domain translation of the same Greek, in this order: Ottley's Isaiah from Codex Alexandrinus (1909) for Esaias; the King James Version where the Hebrew reads with Alexandrinus; the Revised Version of 1885 where the King James Version does not render the idiom. Borrowed wording is adapted into King James English. No variant Brenton did not report is translated.
- The displaced words go into a note under their true witness: `Vat.` where Swete's Vaticanus main text has the Greek they render, however freely, a name in its usual English form included; `Valpy` where Valpy's printed edition, which Brenton translated, has that Greek and B differs or is missing; `Brenton` only for his own English that neither has; `Heb. agrees.` (or `nearly agrees`) first where Brenton records that the Hebrew reads with Alexandrinus, with his "compare the Heb." kept after the displaced words. An addition is `Valpy omits` where Valpy lacks the words and B does not establish the omission, `Brenton omits` where neither does.
- The resulting English must render the Alexandrine construction in context. Swete’s confirmation of the words Brenton reports does not by itself establish that his gloss can replace the corresponding words in his text; `agrees` records the evidence, not eligibility for promotion. Keep the report as a note, with the reason in its decision, where promotion would require an unreported change or leave a construction the permitted English sources do not support. The reading needs no other reading Brenton did not report to complete its sentence. The Appendix's stone command stays in the note on 3 Kingdoms 5:30: printing it would leave the dated sentence of 6:1 unfinished, which Alexandrinus completes with a clause Brenton did not report.
- The words go where Alexandrinus has them, under the source's verse numbers before Assemble. A passage Alexandrinus orders otherwise prints in its order, keeping its numbers, with a note at each place saying where Brenton has it (3 Kingdoms 7:38–50 before 7:1). A verse Alexandrinus lacks is omitted and quoted in a note on the verse before (2 Kingdoms 5:16a).
- A passage from the Appendix prints in its book alone, with Brenton's footnotes within it set on the supplied words. The three passages Brenton announced without complete English (1 Kingdoms 23:12, 3 Kingdoms 14:1–20, Ezekiel 1:14) use the King James Version, with Brenton's names, conformed to Alexandrinus only by declared edits and identified in a note at the passage. Displaced King James words are labelled as the Hebrew's where the Hebrew supports them, and otherwise as the King James Version's.
- Every word an edit of a King James passage supplies must stand in the original King James passage or in a Brenton verse the decision names under `supplied_sources` (its `source`, `reference`, and `why`), read before promotion and without its notes. The build refuses a supplied word the King James passage already has, a word no source gives, a source that licenses nothing, a duplicate source, and `supplied_sources` outside a King James passage.
- Render grammatical irregularities in context and explain them in notes. The omission of 3 Kingdoms 12:24a–z is the omission of the whole Vatican addition, not a relocation of all its contents to chapter 14; its note on 12:24 introduces the passage and gives it in full after its own words, each verse under its number, as the note on 3 Kingdoms 5:14 gives 5:14a–b.

Ordinary witness notes are composed from the decision: `label` names a terminology key (Brenton by default), `omits: true` reports an omission instead of quoting the displaced `from` words, and `hebrew_agrees: true` prefixes “Heb. agrees.” These fields preserve the evidence declared by the decision; composing an attribution establishes none. They cannot accompany an explicit `note`. A reading with a declared `lemma` sets its note before the lemma’s first word, wherever the verse has it once, or at the occurrence that meets the words the reading prints. Without one, `note_at` places the caller in the English the reading prints, and the lemma is the words printed after it, unless the lemma is null, for a note about no words; a decision gives `note_at` with a declared lemma only to be refused. A further replacement inherits its parent's note description unless it supplies one of these fields or its own `note`, which replaces that description as a whole. An edit of borrowed King James words must name its witness explicitly or give its own note. The build refuses a composed alternative whose lemma is not every word printed in place of the displaced ones, a composed omission with no printed words to name, and a composed note on a verse omitted whole.

Displaced words that substitute for the whole lemma use `fqa`, an alternative rendering; when annotation widens a repeated lemma, it widens the alternative with the same surrounding words. A quotation within commentary uses `fq` and keeps its own words. Explicit notes remain where composition would lose information: partial alternatives (such as 1 Kingdoms 15:12), source brackets or different punctuation (2 Kingdoms 17:8 and 4 Kingdoms 3:21), qualified hands, near agreement, multiple witnesses or renderings, Greek or Hebrew glosses, citations, and compound remarks. Notes identifying an inserted verse or a range, quoting an omitted verse, or describing a relocation remain explicit or use their existing structural composers; these are commentary about a passage rather than whole-lemma alternatives.

## Notes

Every note stands in the inside margin beside its verse, without a caller, beginning with the verse's number and the words it is about, followed by a colon. Notes too tall for a page's margin are moved below the text by `edition/margin.json`, keyed by the note, with lettered callers restarting on each page; Brenton's Appendix notes and the prefaces' notes are footnotes (lowercase letters in scripture, numbers in front matter).

> **20** I doubted of such manner of questions: or, *I was doubtful how to enquire hereof*

Check, in both testaments:

- Alternative renderings, cited words, and glosses are italic, without quotation marks; labels, references, and comments are roman.
- A note runs on from its colon in lowercase, except for names and the opening capital of a quotation. A complete sentence is capitalized and punctuated as one; a fragment has no final full stop.
- The lemma retains the verse’s internal punctuation, including a colon before the colon that introduces the note (Luke 4:41, Revelation 14:13, Haggai 2:14). A separate verse-specific revision may change both verse and lemma, as the retained comma decision does at Luke 1:45; two colons alone do not require such a revision.
- The lemma quotes enough of the verse to be unambiguous; if that takes more words, the rendering takes the same words ("of your Father: or, *with your Father*", Matthew 6:1).
- Liskov substitution principle: an alternative rendering must be able to replace the words it is about in the verse without breaking the grammar of the surrounding sentence.
- Brenton's source sets labels and cited words alike in italic; the edition tells them apart. Scholars' names are written out. In scripture itself a correction may mend only word spacing or restore a whole note Brenton printed that the transcription lacks.
- Each note is placed on the words it is about: the words it renders if it gives another rendering, otherwise its clause or its verse.
- A note stands before the first word it is about, whatever context the printed lemma adds around those words. The build places every note whose words are declared apart from its caller: a Brenton or 1611 lemma override moves its note there, wherever Brenton's or George's caller stood, and a reading's declared lemma, a note preserved through a reading's change, and a Received Text note are set there. Words it cannot find once stop the build and name the note. A null lemma or an automatically inferred scope retains its existing position.
- Declare the words actually glossed, with their occurrence where repeated; let annotation add any disambiguating context to the printed lemma and echo it in whole-lemma alternatives. Context needed for a spelling revision does not enlarge the scope: an existing note-specific revision keeps a narrow lemma in agreement with its verse. Lexical explanations and abbreviated glosses remain quotations within commentary rather than incomplete whole-lemma alternatives.
- The edition writes notes of four kinds only: the Vatican, Valpy, or Brenton reading displaced by Alexandrinus, the Received Text reading displaced by the Byzantine text, the former King James words of a correction, and where a passage stands. It adds no commentary or gloss, and a change that only marks or unmarks a supplied word carries no note.

### The Received Text's readings

- A change of words for a difference of Greek carries a note labelled `TR` where Boyd's apparatus or the printed RP2026's gives the reading as TR (Scrivener and Stephanus agreeing) and neither as SCR (Scrivener alone); otherwise the printed edition the translators followed (`Beza`, `Erasmus`, `Stephanus 1549`, `Complutensian text`, `Aldine text`), where its text or Scrivener's account of their sources shows it, as an Old Testament note names `Valpy`; and `KJV` only where none is shown, as an Old Testament note is `Brenton`.
- Shapes: "fruit: TR *fruits*" replaces the lemma; "said: TR adds *by them of old time*" puts words after it; "verily: TR omits" takes it out; "but who: TR omits *but*" takes out part of it. An omitted verse is quoted whole in a note on the verse before it; a moved passage is noted at both its places.
- A correction of English where the Greek is shared gives the former words after "or," with no witness ("in thy kingdom: or, *into thy kingdom*").
- [The New Testament guide](new-testament.md#the-notes) says how the lemma is chosen and when a 1611 note is left out or reversed.

### The 1611 marginal notes

- The source listing has 775 New Testament notes (Scrivener's own count was 765); George gives the words each note is about, and the note is placed on those words. Only the New Testament notes are used.
- A note whose alternative is now printed, or whose lemma has disappeared, is accounted for by a decision in `edition/kjv-notes.json` with its reason; some are reversed to quote the former English.
- If a note still applies after its words change, place it on the new words. Keep the translators' remark about the missing Luke 17:36 at 17:35 beside the note quoting that verse.
- George's bracketed remarks are removed; the Greek he left out at Acts 13:18 and 13:34 is restored; the notes listed under 1 Corinthians 10:6 and Galatians 4:24 stand at 10:4 and 4:25; "began to wept" (Mark 14:72) and "Or, hell" (Revelation 20:13) are corrected as later printings have them.

### Brenton's notes and Appendix

- Brenton's notes come from eBible, supplemented from CCEL ([sources/README.md](../sources/README.md#ccel-brenton-html-edition)); the CCEL review lists placements, duplicates, and omissions.
- A note of the 1844 printing that the later Bagster printings dropped is restored at its 1844 caller, and information they shortened is recovered from its 1844 wording, retaining genuine corrections in later printings. Check disagreements against the printed sources, including Swete's images where the Greek is at issue. The correction in `edition/brenton-notes.json` cites the pages of each printing and explains the choice; the scripture keeps eBible's words.
- The Appendix is not printed as a unit: its passages stand in their books; its eight notes (2 Kingdoms 5:18, Job 25:2 and 39:13, Psalm 41:5, Proverbs 8:5, Esaias 2:6, Ezekiel 16:44, Exodus 28) are footnotes at their verses with a lettered caller before the word discussed or at the start of the verse; margin notes that only sent the reader to the Appendix are left out, and the pointer is dropped from those that say more; the two 1844 margin notes the Bagster printings dropped (Job 25:2, Ezekiel 16:44) are restored, as is the note at Job 39:13 that every printing has and eBible lacks.

## Quotation links

- Both ends are linked at the first verse of each passage, with a gloss by Turpie's class: A and C in words only, "Heb. + LXX"; B, "Heb. ≠ LXX"; D, "LXX ≠ Heb."; C in whole clauses and E, the reference alone. The gloss means the same at both ends.
- Turpie's Greek text is Tischendorf's seventh edition; where the Byzantine text shortens a quotation (Romans 13:9, Hebrews 2:7, 12:20, Acts 7:37) the link stands. Ten of his 282 entries are left out: five for which he names no settled Old Testament passage (1 Timothy 5:18 among them), three he himself says are not quotations, one he repeats, and Matthew 27:35, whose quotation the Byzantine text lacks.
- The classes judge Turpie's underlying texts, not the edition's revised English or every promoted reading. An agreement gloss does not promise identical English. Class C in words only joins A; whole-clause departures and joined passages receive the reference alone, without a claim of agreement.
- Brenton's "See" cross-reference is dropped where the link of a passage that takes in its verse names the same verses, and usually kept where it names more; a note that says more than its reference keeps the rest of its words without it. His note at Psalm 21:19 is kept.

## Citations and abbreviations

- Every citation is printed one way: abbreviated book name, colon between chapter and verse, en dash in a range, comma between verses, semicolon between books, naming the book and verse as this edition does; the words around it are the source's ("See Heb. 2:6–9"; "See 2 Kgdms. 22:16"; "See verse 3"). One psalm is "Ps." and several "Pss.".
- Brenton cites by his printed numbers and the translators of 1611 by the King James Bible's; both are converted, with `edition/citations.json` saying which verse is meant where the numberings differ.
- Abbreviations and numbers follow *The Chicago Manual of Style*: initialisms without periods; "lit." lowercase; version names in full except Alex., Vat., Heb., LXX, TR, and KJV, and on the website RP26 and OLEB; a note labels the King James Version KJV however Brenton writes it (A. V., Eng. Ver., English Version, English Bible), while front-matter prose keeps "Authorized Version"; an abbreviation used once or twice is printed in full.
- Brenton's addition and omission signs are written "adds" and "omits"; his dash is the sign of omission only where spaced on both sides; a dash in a CCEL note is never read as the sign.
- Every abbreviation printed is in the List of Abbreviations, except the familiar St.; its chapter tables the list from `edition/abbreviations.json` in one alphabet. The textual variants on the website also use RP26 for the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont (2026) and OLEB for this edition; the book's list leaves these out, as it prints neither.

## Spelling, vocabulary, and punctuation

- Whole-word vocabulary changes in `edition/revisions.json` apply wherever the word is printed, notes and front and back matter included (Ghost → Spirit, ghost → spirit, Hallelujah → Alleluia, amongst → among, and the possessive and article rules below), preserving capitalization and surrounding wording; generated quotations and the textual variants use the revised wording. Modernizations and the distinctions of sense listed below apply throughout.
- The King James inflections and past tenses carried into Brenton's Scripture (says → saith, has → hath, spoke → spake) take a scope of `scripture`: they change numbered verses, Psalm titles, and note lemmas and readings. Headings, introductions, and commentary keep their source grammar. A change confined to named notes revises only their quoted readings; a word that is elsewhere a noun lists the notes it changes in.
- Among roman words, the supplied-word marker `\add` does not prevent compounds from joining: `any \add thing\add*` becomes `anything`. Within italic or other visibly distinct styles, it still preserves the division.
- Keep the singular thou/thee and plural ye/you distinction, the old verb inflections, and deliberate archaic vocabulary. Age alone warrants no replacement: unto, wherefore, let meaning hinder, conversation meaning conduct, and prevent meaning go before stay. Keep propitiation and atonement; theological preference alone is no vocabulary rule.
- Five past tenses are carried through Brenton's scripture and note readings as the inflections are: spoke, begot, bore, broke, and swore become spake, begat, bare, brake, and sware, forms the King James canon holds to without exception and uses constantly (spake 631, begat 228, bare 208, sware 84, brake 77; the Apocrypha's two swore and one broke follow); the participles stand, and so does bore meaning pierce. The King James Bible is as constant in tare, forgat, trode, and ware (4, 9, 8, 1 as a past tense) but has them in a handful of verses, and these four are modernized wherever either Bible has them (Mark 9:20, Luke 8:27, 9:42, 12:1, Wisdom 19:20, Judith 8:5), as strake sail is; ware meaning aware is another word and stands, so the verb is revised by its phrases. The choice is of which forms to carry, drawn where the counts leave a gap, between brake at 77 and forgat at 9. A verb it mixes (drave/drove, gat/got, sang/sung) stays each translator's own; the King James strake sail is modernized by its own decision. Keep builded, digged, holpen, and fishes; do not extend the inflection rules into general modernization.
- Two rules decide a current spelling: a form both translations write and neither writes otherwise stands (honour, labour, Saviour, fulness); where they differ or either wavers, one form serves both, the King James Bible's where it keeps to one (inquire, gray, wagon, enterprise) and otherwise the usual form (among, connection). A form English has given up is modernized wherever it stands, whichever Bible has it and however constantly (shew, stedfast, expence, scrole, shipwrack, dulness). A transliterated word is a name, not a spelling, and follows the names rules: Brenton's theraphin (θεραφείν) stands, since the King James teraphim renders the Hebrew and the King James Greek books lack the word; a Hebrew form never displaces a Greek one. Count both source archives before stating a rule, and put the counts in the decision's why. Revise spellings and hyphens or compounds only through declared rules, including sense-dependent inflections. Retain shamefastness and carry shamefast into Brenton's Apocrypha; do not substitute the later shamefacedness. Spelling rules must not conceal a new rendering; the declared Numbers 21:5 misprint corrects ought to out from the 1844 printing.
- Revise compounds only where `edition/revisions.json` declares a change. The common compounds revised here take the form current English gives them: closed where English has closed it (today, anything, beforehand, tonight, birthday, breastplate, honeycomb, storehouse); hyphenated as a modifier before its noun and open after it (sweet-smelling savour, whole-burnt offering, free-will offering; well pleasing to God), and hyphenated in every position where English keeps the hyphen (stiff-necked, like-minded, high-minded, double-tongued, long-suffering, mother-in-law); other declared changes open compounds (fig tree, high priest, Sabbath day, barley harvest, fellow servant; every offering of the Law: burnt, sin, peace, meat, drink, heave, wave, trespass, thank offering; burnt sacrifice). The King James Bible's open forms (fig tree 40, high priest 124, the offerings without exception) are evidence that these are the ordinary forms, not the rule: it also writes mercy seat, dwelling place, and corner stone open, and closes fellowservant and longsuffering. By editorial preference, retain the hyphen in first-born, first-fruits, corner-stone, mercy-seat, stumbling-block, stumbling-stone, threshing-floor, dwelling-place, ewe-lamb, turtle-dove, pre-eminence, battle-array, and well-pleased, with their plurals, in every position. Close numbered compounds in -fold (twofold, fourfold, sevenfold, hundredfold, thousandfold) and write lawgiver and all-wise, with round about open. Hyphenate only-begotten before a noun; retain the noun phrase the only begotten of the Father. Compounds outside the declared changes keep their sources’ forms, including differences between the translations. These rules change no word.
- Capitals by rule: King immediately before a ruler's name (King David), lowercase in generic references and after the name; Mount where it begins a mountain's name (Mount Sinai), and the established name the Mount of Olives; both lowercase where the word is a common noun standing before the name after an article or a demonstrative (the king Abimelech, that king Cyrus, the mount Sion, as the river Jordan), mount after an adjective too, and in descriptive phrases (the mount of God); Sabbath and Sabbaths, Scripture and Scriptures, always.
- Possessives and articles follow the immediately following sound: "my heart", "thy house", "mine eyes", "thine arm", "mine honour", "mine old age", "thy union"; "a house", "a hundred", "a historical account", "an hour", "an heir", "a union", "an unholy man". Independent possessives stay. Capitalization is a separate decision.
- Word pairs are sorted by sense wherever they are printed:

  - "besides" for "in addition" or "other than" ("other gods besides me"), "beside" for "by the side of" and in idioms ("beside himself");
  - "born" of birth, "borne" for bearing ("I have borne a child in mine old age");
  - "farther" for distance, "further" for continuation or addition;
  - "sometime" for formerly, "sometimes" for occasionally, "some time" for a span of time;
  - "diverse" for different in kind or quality ("diverse seed", "diverse weights"), "divers" for various or several;
  - "clothes" for garments, "cloths" for pieces of cloth.

- Names are decisions by bearer. Start from the Greek names familiar in the New Testament and the services; prefer the established anglicized form of the KJV's New Testament or Apocrypha for the same bearer in both Testaments; retain Brenton's form where the King James tradition supplies none. Departures for recognizability, Greek spelling, or service-book usage each need a reasoned decision in `names`, not a new global rule; a book heading imposes no global substitution. In the prefaces and Brenton's Introduction, use Esaias, Aggeus, Esdras, and Zorobabel where their decisions list them under `matter`. Their other names keep their source forms, except that Brenton's Preface follows his Introduction in writing Aristeas.
- A name decision applies only to its identified bearer, with every changed and kept occurrence declared; a verse that names two bearers alike lists each occurrence by number (`LUK 6:16#1`). Josias's father becomes Amon, without changing the prophet Amos or Luke's ancestor; Jesus names the son of Navi alone; Hannah names Samuel's mother alone. Where the Greek gives one bearer two names, keep both: Azarias and Ozias stay distinct for the king of Judah, and Tabor and Itabyrion for the mountain (Ἰταβύριον at Hos 5:1 and Jer 26:18), each Greek name in one English form.
- A respelling that changes only a name's form reaches every bearer to whom the Greek and the Hebrew both give that name, because the split exists in neither Scripture: Hebron the Levite as Hebron the city, Sedecias the false prophet as Sedecias the king, Esrom Reuben's son as Esrom Phares's son (both Hezron; Ἁσρών for both in Swete's Pentateuch), Caleb son of Esrom as Caleb son of Jephunne, Tabor the oak and the town as Tabor the mountain. A bearer whose Hebrew name differs, whom the Greek translator alone assimilated, keeps the source form, since the identification is the translator's and the English reader knows him by another name: Kedron of Zabulon (Kitron) beside Cedron the brook, Charrhan at Gen 36:26 (Cheran) and Amos 1:5 (Kir), Josia for Joshah, Joshaviah, and Jeshaiah, Thecue at 3 Kgdms 10:28 (Kue), Chaleb for Chelubai and Chelub. State the Hebrew name in the keep's who.
- Where the Greek gives two bearers two names, keep the difference: Phineas and Phinees (Φινόε) at 1 Esd 5:31, Phoenicia and Phoenix of Crete (Φοῖνιξ), Salmanasar and Enemessar. Declension is grammar, not a second name: Brenton's Manasse for the tribe (Μανασσή, never declined) and Manasses for the king (Μανασσῆς in half his verses, Μανασσή in the rest) merge to Manasses, the King James form at Matt 1:10 and Rev 7:6, as Josias and Sedecias each merge their declined and undeclined forms. Two Greek spellings of one bearer are one name: Ἀμών and Ἀμώς are Amon, and Ῥαχάβ at Matt 1:5 and Ῥαάβ at Heb 11:31 are Rahab. Before merging two source spellings, tabulate the Greek of Swete and of Scrivener at every verse the decision lists under forms and keep; two bearers are kept apart only where the Greek gives them distinct names (Φινεές and Φινόε, Φοινίκη and Φοῖνιξ), never where it merely declines one name and not the other, or declines it in some verses and not others.
- Where the King James Bible names one bearer two ways and the cited Orthodox tradition identifies him, one name, with that tradition specified in the decision: Jude for the apostle and the Lord's brother (June 19); Mark for the Evangelist at Col 4:10, Philemon 24, and 1 Pet 5:13, as in Acts (following the April 25 Synaxarion’s identification; Eusebius 2.15, 3.39).
- Judah, the King James Apocrypha's form at Sir 49:4, for the patriarch, his tribe, and every bearer Brenton writes Juda, chosen over Juda and Judas for recognizability; the men the sources write Judas (Maccabaeus, Simon's son, the Levite of Ezra 10:23, the New Testament's other Judases) keep it.
- The readings and remarks of Brenton's own notes keep their source names, since a name there is often the note's subject.
- The ligatures æ and œ are written out as ae and oe wherever the translations, their notes, their front and back matter, and the edition's own pages print them, in names and Latin alike: Brenton's Thæman and Phœnicia are printed Thaeman and Phoenicia. This is done after every other revision, so the decisions name the forms the sources print. A respelling or verse change that writes a ligature, or that does no more than write one out, is refused.
- Punctuation by rule: a comma, semicolon, or colon that duplicates the break another mark makes is removed before an em dash and before a parenthetical aside (at a verse's end too when the next verse opens one); inside a closing parenthesis it moves after it, or goes when a dash follows. A parenthesized number or single lowercase letter marks a reference or list item rather than an aside, so its preceding punctuation stays, across lines and verses too. Periods, question marks, and exclamation marks are preserved by rule. Brenton's six pairs of quotation marks in scripture are removed. The pair around the city's name that Alexandrinus adds at Ezekiel 48:35, ‘the Lord is there’, stays as a named exception: the name is a clause standing as the subject of shall be the name thereof, and the marks keep its verb apart from the sentence's.
- A verse-specific punctuation change, or a change to a keyed note or a front-matter paragraph, is a decision under `verses` or `passages`, matching its plain text exactly once.
- Such a decision changes punctuation, spacing, and sentence-initial capitals only, unless its group of verse changes declares the one kind of wording it changes: `spelling` (the same word in another spelling or inflection, chosen by sense verse by verse: the word pairs above, pities/pitieth) or `misprint` (a word the source's printing got wrong, which another printing shows). A declared group changes one word a change; the New Testament admits neither kind. A `passages` change may instead declare `spelling` as `greek` or `hebrew`, correcting the printed Greek or Hebrew of a note or of front matter while preserving its English and its witness.
- Stale, overlapping, and unused decisions stop the build.
- Revision runs after source-dependent decisions, so a lemma or a `from` names "Jezekiel" if the source does; the edition's own pages quote the revised text and receive the punctuation pass while keeping the source names they discuss.
- Authored prose may quote source forms and before-and-after examples to discuss them; preserve those examples instead of applying scripture's revisions blindly, except that the ligature write-out runs on the edition's own pages too, so an example cannot show a ligature. New Testament question marks follow RP2026 where its pointing differs from the Received Text, through declared decisions rather than a general punctuation rewrite.

## Typography

- Supplied words (`\add`) are set in ordinary type; in an italic title they are italic with the rest.
- Book titles, section headings, kathisma and stasis headings, chapter labels, and Sirach's Prologue heading are regular small capitals, as the running heads are; subtitles and descriptive titles are italic, as is Psalm 118's "Middle." Chapter numbers use lining figures. Lamentations' acrostic names are centered small-capital headings.
- Inline small capitals mark decorative openings alone; divine names, scholarly names, and Aramaic speech use ordinary type. A complete highlighted prophetic title takes `\d`; a highlighted fragment within a sentence stays prose.
- Emphatic capitals in scripture and the prefaces are set in ordinary contextual case (Jesus, God, the Being, the Name, I am, the inscriptions); abbreviations, sigla, and Roman numerals keep their capitals.
- Brenton's square brackets are not printed: in a book the Hebrew has they marked Greek the Hebrew lacks, and the explanatory notes stay on those words; in a book it lacks they marked supplied English, which is marked `\add`. Exodus 5:14 and Judges 1:10 are the two other cases, handled as the `brackets` rule in `edition/revisions.json` records.
- Direct speech receives no added quotation marks; existing quotation marks in prose are typographic; in notes italic takes their place around cited words.
- Greek is set in GFS Heraklit with Valkyrie and in GFS Didot with Utopia; Hebrew is set in Ezra SIL with either. [font-normalization.md](font-normalization.md) explains the assembly.
- Print on ISO B5 (176 × 250 mm), with a single scripture column and inside-margin notes. The published face is Valkyrie through OLEBFont, at 9 pt on an 11 pt baseline grid registered front and back; Utopia is the fallback. Chapter figures are large and lining, set in TeX Gyre PagellaX with Valkyrie and in Utopia's own figures with Utopia; other figures are oldstyle, and verse figures superior; omit verse one beside the chapter figure. The outer running head gives the first reference on even pages and the last on odd pages. Add no editorial section headings or cross-reference column.

## Lines of verse and paragraphs

- The Old Testament sets a verse in lines where the Updated Brenton does and Scrivener's King James counterpart is in lines too or has no say (no counterpart, or the Apocrypha). A mapped counterpart set as prose supplies a negative judgment, even in a wholly prose book; a lettered addition goes as its verse goes. Ecclesiastes 3:9 and Sirach 50:27 are explicit prose exceptions under `prose` in `edition/lines.json`; an exception that suppresses no otherwise retained poetry stops the build.
- Alignment is word by word; a break stands before the matched first word of a line or after the matched last word of the preceding line; the few breaks alignment cannot place are decisions in `edition/lines.json`, each naming the update line and the edition's line's opening words once. A line the words cannot place needs no decision where its verse and every verse it can begin in are prose; a decision that sets a line in a prose verse where it would stand anyway is refused. The update supplies no wording, spelling, punctuation, headings, or numbering.
- Paragraph decisions act only at existing prose boundaries, named by an exact opening phrase in the assembled, unrevised verse; continuations and repeated labels may be flush left (`m`), new narrative or discourse units are `p`; a change of speaker alone does not open a new unit. Each chapter's prose opens a new paragraph; blank lines mark stanza breaks and turns between poetry and prose, with `unspaced` for any exception. An acrostic label adds no stanza break within the poem.
- At a poetry/prose transition across chapters, the blank stands before the chapter figure. Other chapter boundaries, headings, titles, acrostic names, divisions, and lines following a "Pause." instruction receive no blank padding. Scripture's `\b` retains normal paragraph indentation; only introductory `\ib` suppresses the following indent.
- "Pause." ending a poetic line of the Psalms or Habakkuk, after a punctuated clause or alone, is `\qs` on its own right-aligned line. It keeps its explanatory note, its own punctuation (Psalm 67:8 closes with a colon), and the song it belongs to (Psalm 9:17's "A song of Pause.").
- Lamentations' acrostic names are `\qa` headings whose terminal periods the rule `acrostic-headings` removes; the three names Brenton sets inside their verses are those the line decisions head a line with. In the document a verse milestone stays before an acrostic name; on the page the verse's number prints beside the `q1` line that follows the name, and at a chapter's opening the chapter figure stands in its place as usual.
- Neither rule supplies words or Hebrew letters. Check the labels' spellings, order, verse placements, and chapter counts against the pinned source.
- Print sets a line opening a verse, poem, or stanza at `q1` and a continuation at `q2`. Their first physical lines start at twice and three times the ordinary prose paragraph indent, respectively; wrapped continuations of either level start at six times that indent. A verse number opening a poetic line hangs as PTXprint sets it; beside a dropped chapter figure and within a poetic line, verse numbers remain inline.
- The New Testament keeps Scrivener's paragraphs and his poetry (the Beatitudes, the canticles of Luke 1–2, and Revelation 7 and 18).
- Susanna and Bel are headed by chapter labels (`\cl`), Sirach's Prologue is an introduction (`\imt1`, `\ip`), the Epistle of Jeremias's title is `\d`, the Song of the Three Children is `\s1`; the Psalter marks kathismata `\ms1`, named stases `\ms2`, other stases `\sd2`, and its middle `\lit`; the four Gospels’ and fourteen epistles’ subscriptions are colophons (`\esb`).
- Keep the Psalter's 20 kathismata and 60 stases under Septuagint numbering, with Psalm 151 outside them. Mark ordinary stases with a rule; in Psalm 118 name the Second and Third Stases and the Middle at verse 94. Psalm 71:20 remains a line of the psalm, not a detached colophon.

## Front matter

- All introductory matter precedes the Old Testament divider as `\periph` divisions: Acknowledgments, the editor's Introduction, Preface to the Old Testament, Introduction to the Old Testament, the Epistle Dedicatory, Preface to the New Testament, and List of Abbreviations. Each opens on a recto with a centered lowercase Roman folio; later folios stand at the outer corners. Marker decisions in `edition/prose.json` set title lines, block quotations, subtitles, list entries, and divisions; other blank lines are dropped; wording is kept apart from recorded omissions; citations, abbreviations, and book names follow the edition's style.
- Brenton's introduction is completed from the 1851 printing's pp. viii–xiii, stopping before its "Apocrypha" heading, by a restoration in `edition/prose.json` with an exact anchor; `sources/brenton1851.pdf` is pinned by checksum and not OCRed.
- Left out: eBible's list of corrections, its introduction to the Apocrypha, Brenton's table of the chapters of Jeremias (the website's tables replace it), and the Cambridge Paragraph Bible's 1873 title page.
- The website also prints the preliminary chapters under Read; website-only are the tables of chapters and verses and the textual variants.
- The sources' prefaces and introductions retain their authors' opinions. Scrivener's critical introduction and appendices are not part of the electronic source and are not printed. Testament divider pages are print-only; the website's reader sections stand for them. Each divider is a recto without a folio, backed by a blank page, and its testament's first book opens on the next recto.
- The introduction's book-name and quotation-gloss tables, and the separate alphabetized List of Abbreviations, are generated from the registries. The website's textual variants give both Greek readings and, where English changes, both English readings. Keep derived quotations, tables, and current reconciliation counts out of hand-maintained decisions; use the generated review to check them.
