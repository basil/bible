# Editorial notes

The [README](../README.md) describes what is in this Bible. This page gives the rules by which it was edited, and the reasons for them. [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md) explains how the build carries them out, and [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md) how each kind of decision is reviewed.

The edition is the Orthodox Liturgical English Bible, or OLEB. It is Orthodox because its Old Testament is the Septuagint, in the Church of Greece's order. It is liturgical because both translations are in the English of the King James Bible, which English translations of the Orthodox services have commonly used.

The translations are preserved with a few exceptions. In the Old Testament, some readings from Codex Alexandrinus are printed in the text, two missing passages are supplied from the King James Version, and spelling and punctuation are revised. At Psalm 67:16, Brenton’s full “curdled like cheese” gloss replaces “swelling”, with the former rendering retained in its source note; both render the same Greek. Slips in the transcriptions are corrected. In the New Testament, the King James wording is conformed to the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont (2026) wherever that text differs from the Received Text in a way English can show, and every change carries a note giving the Received Text's words; [the New Testament text](new-testament.md) gives the account. Around the text, the two sources are brought into one style: book names, notes, citations and abbreviations. Whatever is doubtful stays as the source has it.

## Texts

- **Old Testament:** [eBible's transcription of Brenton](https://ebible.org/eng-Brenton/), which includes eBible's corrections to the 1870 printing. It is neither a facsimile nor the separate "Updated Brenton" translation, whose words are not printed; its lines of verse are, as [the lines of verse](#lines-of-verse) explains.
- **New Testament:** [eBible's Cambridge Paragraph Bible](https://ebible.org/engkjvcpb/), not its standard KJV. Only the 27 books, the translators' dedication to King James, and their preface are used. The text keeps Scrivener's paragraphs and poetry layout, and is conformed to the Byzantine text as [the New Testament text](new-testament.md) describes, from the Greek texts and witnesses listed in [sources/README.md](../sources/README.md#the-new-testament-greek-and-its-witnesses).
- **Marginal notes:** [Calvin George's transcription](https://en.literaturabautista.com/exhaustive-listing-marginal-notes-1611-edition-king-james-bible) of the notes in the 1611 King James Bible. Only the New Testament notes are used. The Old Testament notes belong to the KJV's translation from the Hebrew, which this edition does not print.

[sources/README.md](../sources/README.md) has the retrieval dates and original copyright notices, and the [README](../README.md#license) gives the license.

## Order and names

The Old Testament follows the [Church of Greece's list of books](https://apostoliki-diakonia.gr/bible/zacharias/). The books that English Bibles set apart as the Apocrypha stay in their Greek places, so there is no separate Apocrypha section.

Brenton's book names are kept, with two kinds of exception. The Orthodox names are used for Jesus, the Son of Navi; 1–4 Kingdoms; the Song of Songs; the Lamentations of Jeremias; the Epistle of Jeremias; Michaias; Abdias; and Abbacum. The familiar English names are used for Ezekiel, Hosea and Nahum. Numbered books put the number first, as in "1 Chronicles".

Every book has a full title in the style of the Cambridge Paragraph Bible, adapted to this edition's names and extended to the books the Cambridge Bible lacks: "The Book of Jesus, the Son of Navi", "The Third Book of Kingdoms, Otherwise Called, The First Book of the Kings". These replace Brenton's own subtitles, such as "(1 SAMUEL)". In the New Testament, "S." is written out as "Saint", and the letters of James, Peter, John, and Jude are called Catholic Epistles, as in the Greek tradition.

Each book also has a short title for the running heads and an abbreviation for citations; the Psalter's running heads name one psalm, as "Psalm", and Susanna and Bel and the Dragon are named by their headings in the running heads and the PDF bookmarks. The contents prints the abbreviation beside the title, and so serves as the key.

## Divisions of the text

The edition numbers Brenton's text as the Septuagint is numbered, as [The Old Testament's numbering](numbering.md) explains. These places are divided otherwise than eBible's transcription or the English Bibles divide them:

- **Ezra and Nehemiah.** eBible's transcription holds them as one book of 23 chapters, which is printed as 2 Esdras (chapters 1–10) and Nehemias (chapters 11–23, numbered 1–13).
- **Daniel.** Susanna comes before Daniel 1 and Bel and the Dragon after Daniel 12, each as a titled section without a chapter number. The Song of the Three Children gets its own heading before 3:25, after the narrative verse that opens it, and is numbered within the chapter as Swete and Rahlfs number it, Daniel 3:24–90. The Church of Greece and Brenton's printing number it on its own as verses 1–67, but the edition keeps the numbers Swete and Rahlfs cite ([The Old Testament's numbering](numbering.md#alternatives-rejected)).
- **Malachias.** eBible numbers the close of the book 3:19–24, as Rahlfs does. It is printed as chapter 4, verses 1–6, as the [Church of Greece's text](https://apostoliki-diakonia.gr/bible/malachias/?file=42.4) and Swete print it. Most English Bibles divide the chapter there too, but order its last three verses otherwise: the Greek has Elias before the law of Moses, so its 4:4–6 are the King James Bible's 4:5, 4:6 and 4:4.
- **Proverbs.** The Greek sets the Hebrew's 30:1–14, 30:15–33 and 31:1–9 within chapter 24, the first after 24:22e and the others after 24:34, so its chapter 31 begins at verse 10. The edition prints these verses under the Hebrew's numbers where they stand, as the Church of Greece, Brenton's printing and Rahlfs do: the first verse of each passage with its chapter, as 30:1, and the first verse back in chapter 24 as 24:23. eBible's transcription numbers an empty chapter 30 with a remark of its own pointing to chapter 24; the edition prints neither. A note at 29:27 says that the Hebrew, and so the King James Bible, has the three passages there, and that they are printed in chapter 24 ("Heb. has here 30:1–14, 30:15–33 and 31:1–9, printed in chapter 24"). The New Testament's moved passages are noted in the same way, with the Received Text's label. The table of chapters and verses gives the rest.
- **Sirach and the Epistle of Jeremias.** Sirach's Prologue is set as the book's introduction, unnumbered, so that the book's first verse is 1:1, as in the Church of Greece's text and Swete. The Epistle's title likewise prints no number, and its verses are 1–72, with no 16 ([The Old Testament's numbering](numbering.md#special-places)).
- **The New Testament.** The Byzantine text lacks four verses of the Received Text, Luke 17:36, Acts 8:37, 15:34 and 24:7, which are omitted, each quoted in a note on the verse before it; it sets the doxology of Romans at 14:24–26 rather than 16:25–27, where it stands with a note at each place ("TR has this passage at 16:25–27", and at 16:24 "TR has here the verses printed at 14:24–26"); and it exchanges Matthew 23:13 and 14, each noted with where the Received Text has it. The table of chapters and verses lists these too.

## Lines of verse

Brenton printed his Old Testament as prose. The edition sets a verse in lines where Adam Boyd's [Updated Brenton](https://ebible.org/englxxup/) (2020) does, provided Scrivener's Cambridge Paragraph Bible sets its King James counterpart in lines too, or has no say. Boyd supplies word-level boundaries because his text can be aligned with Brenton's; Scrivener supplies verse-level classification because the King James translation's wording differs.

Scrivener has a say in the books of the King James Old Testament that he sets partly in lines, wherever a verse has a counterpart there. He has none in books he sets wholly as prose, in verses without a counterpart, or in the Apocrypha, which the table of chapters and verses does not map: there Boyd's lines stand alone, though Scrivener sets only a few prayers and songs of Tobit, Judith and Baruch in lines. Sirach 50:27, the author's colophon, is an explicit prose exception following Scrivener, recorded under `prose` in `edition/lines.json`; an exception that suppresses no otherwise retained poetry stops the build. A Septuagint addition lettered to a verse goes as that verse goes. Thus Job's narrative and most of Ecclesiastes stay prose despite Boyd's lines, while Isaiah and the minor prophets stay prose despite Scrivener's lines.

The build aligns each update chapter word by word with Brenton's, retaining Boyd's line and stanza boundaries. A break stands before the matched first word of a line or after the matched last word of the preceding line. Verse openings retain Brenton's leading Hebrew letters; prose resumes under Boyd's paragraph marker, including his flush-left continuations. Brenton's other prose paragraphs remain. The paragraph decisions in `edition/lines.json` set continuations and repeated labels flush left, retain new narrative or discourse units as ordinary paragraphs, or join an introduction to the prose it completes. They act only at existing prose boundaries, named by an exact opening phrase in the assembled, unrevised verse; a missing, ambiguous, conflicting or redundant decision stops the build. Immediate responses, repeated introductions and closing actions may remain flush left; a change of speaker alone does not open a new unit. Genesis 49:28 begins a new paragraph at the summary that leads into Jacob’s final instructions, following Scrivener. Numbers 24:25 closes Balaam’s encounter and stays flush left, as both witnesses set it. Exodus 15:19 explains the song, and 2 Kingdoms 3:35 resumes the funeral response: both retain Boyd’s flush-left continuation despite Scrivener’s different paragraphing. The update supplies no wording, spelling, punctuation, headings or verse numbering.

After alignment, a terminal standalone “Pause.” instruction in a poetic line of the Psalms or Habakkuk is marked `\qs` and stands on its own right-aligned line (`\qr`), with its explanatory note. “A song of Pause.”, prose and notes retain their form; the “Pause:” Brenton sets within Psalm 67:8 stays inline, also `\qs`; the build refuses any other. Lamentations 1–4 give Brenton’s existing small-cap acrostic names their own headings (`\qa`), retaining their verse association, including names inside verses; the punctuation rule `acrostic-headings` in `edition/revisions.json` then removes their terminal periods, since they stand as headings, not sentences. These source instructions and labels are separated from the poem for clarity; neither rule supplies words or Hebrew letters. The build checks the labels’ spellings, order, verse placements and chapter counts against the pinned source. Print and the website set the number of a verse a name opens beside the `\q1` line that follows the name; where the chapter number drops into that line, it omits verse one, as after every chapter number. An acrostic label adds no stanza break within the poem.

The few breaks the alignment cannot place are decisions in `edition/lines.json`. Each names the update line being followed and the opening words of the edition's line, met once in its source verse, or says that the source has no such line. Unused, repeated, invalid or redundant decisions stop the build.

The New Testament keeps Scrivener's paragraphs and poetry within chapters; [the New Testament text](new-testament.md#lines-of-verse) lists the passages. The documents preserve semantic lines; for print, the view sets a line that opens a verse, a poem or a stanza at USFM’s first poetic level (`q1`), and a line that goes on with a verse at the second (`q2`).

In print, their first physical lines start at twice and three times the ordinary prose paragraph indent, respectively; wrapped continuations of either level start at six times that indent. This restores the upstream base stylesheet’s poetry indentation while retaining PTXprint’s alignment and spacing. At the current 9 pt body size, the prose indent is 7.227 pt (about 0.803 em), giving poetry positions of 14.454 pt, 21.681 pt and 43.362 pt. Line-opening verse numbers have their left edge on the ordinary prose margin, including single-digit numbers and numbers moved from acrostic headings; beside a dropped chapter figure, where PTXprint does not hang them, and within a poetic line, verse numbers remain inline. A label too wide for the inset extends outward while preserving the normal gap before the text.

Each chapter’s prose begins a new paragraph, even where the source continues the preceding chapter’s paragraph or opens flush left; poetry keeps its lines. This applies to print and the website. Blank lines mark stanza breaks and turns between poetry and prose, including across chapter boundaries, where the blank stands before the chapter figure. Ecclesiastes 3:9 resumes prose with the normal blank after the poem of appointed times. Where a decision keeps prose after a poem without that space, `unspaced` in `edition/lines.json` names the verse; the build refuses one that does not open a prose paragraph directly after poetry. Other chapter boundaries get no blank padding, nor do headings, titles, acrostic names or divisions, or lines following a “Pause.” instruction. Print keeps its existing blank-line spacing and indentation rules. The blank-line hook suppresses the indent after introductory `\ib` only; scripture’s `\b` retains the normal paragraph indentation. Numbered ordinary paragraphs can therefore still indent after poetry, as at Genesis 25:24, 49:28 and Exodus 15:22. Flush-left continuations use `m`; new narrative or discourse units retain `p`. Joining Numbers 24:15's introduction to the preceding prose removes an actual paragraph boundary. The website consumes the same paragraph markers but retains its own spacing and currently displays `p` and `m` alike. Neither step changes the words or their capitalization.

Susanna and Bel and the Dragon are headed by chapter labels (`\cl`), Sirach's Prologue is an introduction (`\imt1`, `\ip`), and the Epistle of Jeremias's title, its verse 0, is `\d`. The Song of the Three Children is headed `\s1`. The Psalter marks kathismata `\ms1`, named stases `\ms2`, other stases `\sd2` and its middle `\lit`; the closing notice at 71:20, a title in Brenton, stays a line of the psalm (`\q1`). The subscriptions of fourteen epistles are colophons (`\esb`) after the text, one empty line below it and without their printed pilcrow, and in print they are set across the text's full width without a first-line indent; Acts' closing notice is omitted (`colophons` in `edition/prose.json`).

Every footnote is `\f`, its caller `-` in the margin or `+` below the text. Lemmas are `\fq`, alternative readings `\fqa`, other quotations italic in `\ft`, and citations `\ref` links. In print, a margin note's origin gives only its verse.

## Front matter

Each translation's front matter stands before its own testament, so that the introductions sit next to the text they describe. Each unit is a `\periph` division of an `FRT` or `INT` book. The sources' introduction markers become ordinary paragraphs and headings; decisions in `edition/prose.json` mark what a source leaves unmarked (title lines, block quotations, subtitles, list entries, blank lines between topics), and other blank lines are dropped. Their wording is kept apart from the omissions recorded in `edition/prose.json`; their citations, abbreviations and book names are brought into this edition's style.

- **Old Testament:** Brenton's preface and his introduction.
- **New Testament:** the translators' dedication to King James and their preface, "The Translators to the Reader".
- **On the website too:** these, with Brenton's list of abbreviations and the editor's introduction, each a page under Read where the book has it; the divider pages of the testaments are not pages, the reader's sections standing for them.
- **On the website, and not in the book:** the tables of chapters and verses, a page under Read after the editor's introduction, and the textual variants: every New Testament verse in which the two Greek texts differ, with the Greek of both and, where the English changes, the words of both Bibles. The English changes are based on Pierpont's instructions. Where an instruction cannot be applied or its wording does not fit, a fitting KJV parallel is preferred, then Pierpont, the Revised Version of 1881, and Boyd's Byzantine ASV of 2021; Boyd's Text-Critical English New Testament and Thomason's Far Above All are occasionally consulted, in that order.

Brenton's introduction in eBible ends after the paragraph on early translations from the Septuagint, where the 1870 printing ends it (p. vi in the 1879 reprint of its plates). The 1851 printing goes on; the rest is restored from its [pp. viii–xiii](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/ioscs/brenton/brenton1851.pdf), stopping before the separate “Apocrypha” heading. Where those pages overlap eBible's closing paragraphs, their words agree. The existing opening is retained. The restoration in `edition/prose.json` gives the exact preceding paragraph and the added paragraphs as USJ, with the source's emphasis and blank lines; marker decisions set the early translations as a list and the blank lines before its groups as divisions. The matter stage inserts them before reading citations and terminology, and the usual spelling and punctuation revisions follow. The build refuses a missing or ambiguous anchor. The unchanged scan is pinned as `sources/brenton1851.pdf`: the read stage verifies its checksum, but does not OCR it. Building remains offline.

Brenton's Appendix is not printed as a unit. The passages it supplies from Codex Alexandrinus stand in their books, with the footnotes Brenton prints within them set on the supplied words, and its eight notes (on 2 Kingdoms 5:18, Job 25:2 and 39:13, Psalm 41:5, Proverbs 8:5, Esaias 2:6, Jezekiel 16:44 and the Complutensian verses of Exodus 28) are printed as footnotes at the foot of the page, with a lettered caller before the word discussed or at the start of the verse for notes on the whole verse; the labels that named the verses are dropped. The margin notes that only sent the reader to the Appendix are left out, and the pointer is dropped from those that say more; the three margin notes of 1844 that the Bagster printings dropped in favour of the Appendix (Job 25:2, Job 39:13, Jezekiel 16:44) are restored.

Four things are left out:

- eBible's list of corrections.
- The introduction to the books of the Apocrypha in eBible's transcription.
- Brenton's table of the chapters of Jeremias, which does not match eBible's text. The website's tables of chapters and verses replace it.
- The Cambridge Paragraph Bible's 1873 title page, which advertises an introduction and appendices by Scrivener that are not in the online text.

The traditional notes at the ends of the epistles, such as "Written to the Romans from Corinthus", are kept.

## Readings from Codex Alexandrinus

Brenton translated Valpy's printed Greek text, derived from the Sixtine or Roman edition of 1587. That edition was based chiefly on Codex Vaticanus but supplied its gaps and altered readings from other manuscripts. Brenton gave readings of Codex Alexandrinus in his notes and Appendix. Such a reading is printed in the text where Swete's edition of the Septuagint supports both the reading and its place in the verse, and the English is supported by Brenton or the translations named below. Swete records the manuscript's later corrections along with its first writing, and a corrected reading may be printed. Otherwise the reading stays where Brenton left it.

When a reading is printed:

- The words it displaces become a note marked "Brenton" unless an individual check against Swete establishes the quoted reading for a named manuscript. "Vat." means Codex Vaticanus, with its correcting hand or uncertainty stated where necessary. The default identifies the English source without assigning Valpy’s printed text to a manuscript. An addition is marked "Brenton omits" unless a manuscript’s omission is established. Where Brenton says the Hebrew reads with Alexandrinus, the note begins "Heb. agrees." (or "nearly agrees", where he qualifies it, as his "nearer the Heb." does), with his "compare the Heb." kept after the displaced words.
- The new words normally come from Brenton's English. Where he gives the reading only in Greek, or his English for it is wrong or incomplete, the words his English lacks are supplied from a public-domain translation that renders the same text, and the decision declares each supplied word and names its source: R. R. Ottley's translation of Isaiah from Codex Alexandrinus (1909), or, where the Hebrew reads with Alexandrinus, the King James Version, and the Revised Version of 1885 where the King James Version does not render the idiom. Wording borrowed from Ottley or the Revised Version is adapted into King James English. Variants that Brenton does not report are not translated.
- The words go where Alexandrinus has them, under Brenton's verse numbers. Verses that Alexandrinus orders otherwise are printed in its order, keeping their numbers, with a note at each place saying where Brenton has them, as the New Testament's moved verses are noted: 3 Kingdoms 7:38–50 stand before 7:1. A verse Alexandrinus lacks, where Brenton reports it, is omitted, and its words quoted in a note on the verse before it: 2 Kingdoms 5:16a, and 3 Kingdoms 6:1a, Brenton's base form of the verse printed at 5:31.
- A passage taken from the Appendix prints in its book alone. Two entries of the Appendix's list of supplied passages supplied nothing: Brenton's cross-reference from Proverbs 21:16–17 to his note on 2 Kingdoms 5:18, and his remark at the close of 3 Kingdoms 2 that Alexandrinus varies considerably there rather than omitting. Each stands in the note on its verse.

Two passages are borrowed whole. Brenton's note at 1 Kingdoms 23:12 announces the verse but gives no English, so the verse is taken from the King James Version's 1 Samuel 23:12, and a note says so. His note at 3 Kingdoms 14:21 says that the first twenty verses of the chapter are supplied by Alexandrinus, and his Appendix only that the Vatican text has their substance after 12:24; Swete prints Alexandrinus's Greek entire, which renders the Hebrew, so the verses are taken from the King James Version's 1 Kings 14:1–20, conformed to Alexandrinus in places where it departs from the Hebrew (14:2, 3, 8, 10 and 17) with the King James words in a note marked "Heb.", and a note at 14:1 says so. At 14:10 the King James "him that pisseth against the wall", which Alexandrinus and the Hebrew both have, is given Brenton's "every male", as he renders it in Brenton's base telling at 12:24m, and like him without a note. In both passages the names take Brenton's forms (Keila; Abia, Achia, Selom, Nabat), the King James small capitals go, and spelling is revised as in his text. Brenton’s base form of the story, Brenton's 12:24a–z, stays where he has it.

Doubtful readings are left alone. Genesis 5:2, 5:25 and 6:2 remain notes, because the manuscript's reading stands over an erasure and Swete is unsure of the hand, and so does Genesis 5:26, whose total belongs with 5:25; Job 42:17d and Daniel 7:26 remain notes because Swete reads Alexandrinus only apparently, and Ezekiel 1:9 because its repeated clause stands over an erasure in an uncertain hand and the first hand's text is itself only apparent.

An evident slip of the scribe stays a note too, though Swete is sure of it: a name spelt against the manuscript's own spelling elsewhere, or a number that contradicts its context. Genesis 5:32 and 6:10 keep "Cham", because Alexandrinus writes "Chaph" there alone and "Cham" at every later mention; Numbers 28:24 keeps "seven days", because the corrector's "two days" contradicts the feast that closes on the seventh day at 28:25 and the verse's own "daily"; 2 Kingdoms 8:4 keeps "a thousand" chariots, because Alexandrinus's seven cannot spare the hundred it keeps; and 3 Kingdoms 15:2 keeps Abiu's three years, because sixteen contradicts the chronology of the chapter. Retained source-report discrepancies and the separate witness-attribution work are listed in [the open notes](todo.md).

## Notes

Notes stand in the inside margin beside their verse, without callers, whatever their length or form. PTXprint moves crowded notes up and down within their page but cannot carry them to the next, so where a page's notes overrun its margin, the tallest of them are set below the text by decisions in `edition/margin.json`, keyed by the note, as full-width footnotes with automatic lettered callers, restarting on each page. Each must match one note in the margin. The check after typesetting that every margin note stands within its page names any page that overruns. Both placements use PTXprint’s ordinary note size and leading. Front matter and the notes of Brenton's Appendix keep their footnote placement. Bottom notes print under their callers alone, without verse numbers. Scripture’s bottom notes, including those of Brenton’s Appendix, use lowercase letters so that their callers are distinct from verse figures. Front matter’s footnotes and margin references use numbers. Bottom notes leave a fixed 1 pt gap after the caller, without a padded caller box. PTXprint selects the footnote sequence before generating each caller, and callerless notes write no caller-page records, so they cannot disturb the per-page restart. Margin notes begin with the verse's number and the words they are about, followed by a colon:

> **20** I doubted of such manner of questions: or, *I was doubtful how to enquire hereof*

The same style applies in both testaments:

- Alternative renderings, cited words and glosses are in italic, without quotation marks around them.
- Labels, references and explanatory comments are in roman.
- A note runs on from its colon in lowercase, except for names and the opening capital of a quotation.
- A complete sentence is capitalized and punctuated as one. A fragment has no final full stop.
- A note quotes enough of the verse to be unambiguous. If that takes more words, the same words are added to the rendering: "of: or, *with*" in Matthew 6:1 becomes "of your Father: or, *with your Father*".

For alternative renderings, this convention adheres to the Liskov substitution principle: the rendering in the note must be able to replace the words it is about in the verse without breaking the grammar of the surrounding sentence.

### The Received Text's readings

Where the New Testament's words are changed to the Byzantine text's, a note gives the King James words, marked TR where Boyd's apparatus or the printed RP2026's gives them as TR (Scrivener and Stephanus agreeing) and neither as SCR (Scrivener alone), and otherwise marked KJV, as an Old Testament note is marked Brenton unless Swete establishes a manuscript; in the style above: "fruit: TR *fruits*" replaces the words it is about, "said: TR adds *by them of old time*" puts words after them, "verily: TR omits" takes them out, and "but who: TR omits *but*" takes out a part of them. An omitted verse is quoted in a note on the verse before it, and a moved passage is noted at both its places. Where the King James English is corrected and the Greek is the same in both texts, the note gives the former words after "or," with no witness ("in thy kingdom: or, *into thy kingdom*"). [The New Testament text](new-testament.md#the-notes) explains how the words a note is about are chosen.

### The 1611 marginal notes

The New Testament has the 775 notes of the 1611 King James Bible, less fifteen whose reading is the Byzantine reading the text now prints, or which gloss words of the Received Text that the edition no longer has; each is left out by a decision with its reason. George gives the words each note is about, and the note is placed on those words in its verse.

His text is corrected in a few places. His own bracketed remarks are removed, the Greek he left out at Acts 13:18 and 13:34 is restored from the 1611 margin, and a few slips of transcription are fixed. Two notes that he lists under 1 Corinthians 10:6 and Galatians 4:24 are moved to 10:4 and 4:25, where they belong. Two misprints of the 1611 itself are corrected as later printings have them: "began to wept" at Mark 14:72 becomes "weep", and "Or, hell" at Revelation 20:13 becomes "Or, the grave".

### Brenton's notes

Brenton has 4,427 notes and 150 cross-references: 2,595 notes in eBible's transcription, and 1,832 that it lacks, taken from the Christian Classics Ethereal Library's transcription and set on the same words ([source documentation](../sources/README.md#ccel-brenton-html-edition)). Each note is placed on the words it is about: the words it renders, if it gives another rendering, and otherwise its clause or its verse. The cross-references are treated under [Quotations](#quotations).

Brenton's source sets labels and cited words alike in italic; the edition tells them apart and applies the style above. Slips in the transcription are corrected, and scholars' names are written out. In the scripture itself, corrections are limited to word spacing.

## Quotations

Where the New Testament quotes the Old, both ends are linked: the first verse of the quotation names the passage it quotes, and the first verse of that passage names the quotation. A gloss after the reference says which text the quotation follows.

The judgments are David McCalman Turpie's, from *The Old Testament in the New* (1868). It is in the public domain, covers every quotation, and judges the underlying Hebrew and Greek texts rather than either English version. Turpie gives each quotation one of five classes, A–E, according to its agreement with the Hebrew and the Septuagint ([introductory remarks, p. XVI](https://archive.org/download/oldtestamentinne00turp/oldtestamentinne00turp.pdf#page=22)).

| Turpie's class | Gloss | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A; C in words only | "Heb. + LXX" | Agrees with both, allowing differences in wording |
| B | "Heb. ≠ LXX" | Follows the Hebrew and differs from the Septuagint |
| D | "LXX ≠ Heb." | Follows the Septuagint and differs from the Hebrew |
| C in whole clauses; E | No gloss | Departs from both by whole clauses, or follows neither |

In class C the Hebrew and the Septuagint agree with each other, and the quotation differs from them. Where it differs only in words, it joins A. Where it differs by whole clauses, as when a verse is abridged or two passages are joined, the reference is printed alone, so as not to claim a match. The meaning of a gloss is the same at both ends of the link.

Turpie's Greek New Testament is Tischendorf's seventh edition (1859), a critical text which, where the Byzantine text departs from the Received Text, more often reads with the Byzantine. Where such a departure shortens a quotation, as at Romans 13:9, Hebrews 2:7 and 12:20 and Acts 7:37, the words that remain still quote the passage, the TR note gives the words lost, and his class stands, so the link is kept. Of his 282 entries, ten are left out: eight that he himself doubts or repeats, 1 Timothy 5:18, for which he gives no Old Testament passage, and Matthew 27:35, whose quotation of Psalm 21:19 the Byzantine text lacks: Turpie himself marks it as given in the received text alone, and the edition prints it only in the TR note, so that the verse no longer quotes the psalm. [Kalvesmaki's chart](https://www.kalvesmaki.com/LXX/NTChart.htm) lists 23 more parallels that Turpie does not list. He gives no judgment on them, so they are not linked.

Brenton's 150 cross-references mark quotations too, with a note such as "See Rom. 4. 7,8". Where a link names the same verses, his note is dropped so that the reference is not given twice. A note that says more than a link could, such as a gloss or a pointer to the Hebrew, is usually kept. Brenton's cross-reference at Psalm 21:19 to Matthew 27:35, which no link names now, is kept as he wrote it: the psalm's words stand at that verse in the TR note.

## Numbering

Every reference uses this edition's chapter and verse numbers, because those are the numbers on the page. They are the Septuagint's: eBible's, which are Rahlfs's in most chapters, with the Church of Greece's where a second witness confirms them, and verses divided otherwise where the Greek editions agree and Brenton's English allows. [The Old Testament's numbering](numbering.md) gives the rule, its reasons and the evidence it rests on. A reference written by another numbering is converted: Turpie's Joel 2:28–32 is this edition's 3:1–5.

For readers who look verses up in another Bible, the table after the editor's introduction gives the King James Bible's number for every chapter and verse that it numbers otherwise, and notes the verses that one Bible has and the other lacks. In the New Testament these are the verses the Byzantine text lacks or places elsewhere. The differences are greatest in two books. The Greek numbers most of the psalms one lower, and the edition counts a psalm's title as a verse or two, as Swete and Rahlfs do, and from chapter 25 on the chapters of Jeremias follow a different order. The King James column uses that Bible's names for the books, since those are what a reader will look under. Where the Septuagint adds to a verse, the addition may be set apart as a lettered verse, such as Proverbs 22:8a, which the King James Bible lacks.

[sources/README.md](../sources/README.md#versification) explains how the two numberings are compared.

## Citations and abbreviations

Every citation, in the notes and in the front and back matter, is printed one way: the book's abbreviation, a colon between chapter and verse, an en dash in a range, and a comma between verses. It names the book and the verse as this edition does. The words around it, such as "See" and "Compare", are the source's.

- Brenton's "See Heb. 2. 6-9" is "See Heb. 2:6–9".
- His "2 Kings" is the Second Book of Kingdoms, so "See 2 Kings 22. 16" is "See 2 Kgdms. 22:16".
- A reference within the same book is spelt out: "See ver 3" is "See verse 3".
- One psalm is "Ps." and several are "Pss.".

Brenton cites by his own printed numbers, and the build reads them as eBible numbers the verse. Where the two differ, a decision says which verse is meant: "Comp. Jer. 9. 24" at 1 Kingdoms 2:10 is Brenton's, the King James Bible's and this edition's 9:24, which eBible numbers 9:23. The translators of 1611 cite by the King James Bible's numbers. Both are converted, as under [Numbering](#numbering).

Abbreviations and numbers follow *The Chicago Manual of Style*:

- Initialisms have no periods. Other abbreviations have standard spacing, capitalization and periods. The notes of both testaments use KJV for the King James Version, and the New Testament's notes also use TR for the Received Text, both listed; the textual variants on the website add RP26 for the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont (2026) and OLEB for this edition, which the book's list leaves out, as it prints neither. "lit." is lowercase, and capitalized only where it begins a sentence.
- The names of versions and texts are printed in full, such as the Vulgate, however Brenton writes them, except that a note labels the King James Version KJV however it is written, as Brenton’s "A. V.", "Eng. Ver.", "English Version" and "English Bible" are. Front-matter prose keeps "Authorized Version" in full. The witnesses he cites most keep their labels: "Alex.", "Vat." and "Heb.", with "LXX" for his "Sept.".
- An abbreviation used only once or twice is printed in full where it stands, as in "seven pence halfpenny".
- Brenton's signs for addition and omission are written "adds" and "omits". A dash after a witness is his sign of omission only where he spaces it on both sides, as in "Heb. — Isaac being troubled"; closed up, as in "Heb.—מ", it is punctuation. CCEL's transcription closes the sign up, so a dash in a note of CCEL's is never read as the sign; the omission such a note reports is carried out by its decision in `edition/alexandrinus.json`, which holds back every CCEL note that names Codex Alexandrinus.

Every abbreviation the edition prints is in the list of abbreviations, but the familiar St. Brenton's list gives only twelve, so the others that the notes use are added to it, and the whole list is set in alphabetical order. Turpie's signs, + and ≠, are explained in the editor's introduction, with a table of the glosses the quotation links print, each beside the classes of his that it is printed for and what it means.

## Spelling, vocabulary and punctuation

The edition regularizes spelling and punctuation and records explicit editorial vocabulary changes in `edition/revisions.json`. Throughout the printed edition, including notes and front and back matter, whole-word "ghost" becomes "spirit" and "Ghost" becomes "Spirit": "Holy Ghost" becomes "Holy Spirit", and "yielded up the ghost" becomes "yielded up the spirit". Capitalization and surrounding wording are preserved. Generated quotations and the textual variants use the revised wording.

Possessives follow the immediately following sound: "my heart", "thy house" and "thy holy soul", but "mine eyes", "mine ears", "mine enemies", "thine arm" and "thine inheritance". Silent h takes "mine" or "thine": "mine honour", "thine honourable virginity", "mine hour of need". An intervening adjective governs the form: "mine old age", "Thine ancient mercies", "mine every sin", "mine own country", "thine own soul", "mine own failings". A vowel letter can begin with a consonant sound: "thy union", "thy universal memorial", "thy useless servant", "thy ewe lamb"; contrast "thine unworthy servant". Independent possessives remain: "The silver is mine", "All that I have is thine", "Thine is the kingdom". Capitalization is a separate decision. Luke 2:30 retains "For mine eyes have seen thy salvation".

Articles likewise follow the immediately following sound: "a horse", "a house", "a holy man", "a hundred", "a humble person", but "an hour", "an honour", "an heir". Thus "a union", "a useful thing", "a ewe-lamb", "a eunuch", "a once-barren root" and "Such a one", but "an uncle", "an unknown thing" and "an unholy man"; "a whole hour", but "an hour". The edition prints "a herb", "a historical account", "a uniform" and "a uniformity" by the same principle.

The systematic respellings regularize names. Brenton writes the prophet's name "Jezekiel" at Ezekiel 1:3 and 24:24, transliterating the Greek Ἰεζεκιήλ; the edition prints "Ezekiel". His Osee, Michæas, Obdias, Naum and Ambacum are printed as the edition's titles spell them: Hosea, Michaias, Abdias, Nahum and Abbacum.

Throughout the edition, "amongst" is printed "among". In scripture, where the sources mix the words of a pair, the sense decides:

- "besides" for "in addition" or "other than" ("other gods besides me"), "beside" for "by the side of" and in idioms ("beside himself");
- "born" of birth, "borne" for bearing ("I have borne a child in mine old age");
- "farther" for distance, "further" for continuation or addition;
- "sometime" for formerly, "sometimes" for occasionally, "some time" for a span of time;
- "diverse" for different in kind or quality ("diverse seed", "diverse weights"), "divers" for various or several;
- "clothes" for garments, "cloths" for pieces of cloth.

In English, a comma, semicolon or colon that duplicates the break another mark makes is removed. Adjacent runs of these three marks go before an em dash and before an opening parenthesis, at the end of a verse too when the next verse opens one; inside a closing parenthesis they move after it, or go when a dash follows. Periods (including abbreviation periods), question marks and exclamation marks are preserved by default, so a parenthesis that is its own sentence keeps its period inside. Where the sentence needs a different mark after a parenthesis, or none, or its period after it, that is a decision. The six pairs of quotation marks in Brenton's scripture, five around names and one around speech, are removed: the edition sets names and speech without them. Sentence endings, parenthetical continuations and ordinary-period removals are explicit, reasoned decisions in `edition/revisions.json`: scripture under `verses`, and keyed notes or front/back-matter paragraphs under `passages`. Passage decisions match their plain text exactly once, preserve words and markup, and allow only punctuation, its spacing and sentence-initial capitalization. Stale, overlapping and unused decisions stop the build. The translations are revised after source-dependent decisions; the edition’s own pages quote the revised text and receive the same punctuation treatment while retaining the source names they discuss.

## Typography

- Words the translators supplied, which many Bibles print in italics, are set in ordinary type. In a title set in italic, such as a Psalm's superscription, they are set in italic with the rest.
- Book titles, section headings, kathisma headings and chapter labels are set in regular small capitals, as the running heads are; subtitles and Psalm 118’s stasis headings keep their italic. Chapter numbers and labels use lining figures. Lamentations’ acrostic names are centred small-capital headings without their periods; their verse numbers print beside the following line, while the source verse milestones stay before the names.
- Inline small capitals mark decorative openings alone, including the openings of the sources joined into Daniel and Revelation’s epistolary opening. Divine names, scholarly names and Aramaic speech use ordinary type. Descriptive titles take their style from `\d`, and acrostic labels from `\qa`, without nested small-cap spans, except a title that opens its book, as the Epistle of Jeremias’s does. A complete highlighted prophetic title receives `\d`; a highlighted fragment within a sentence stays prose. The deliberate small-capital heading styles remain in print, and the website styles acrostic headings through `qa`.
- Emphatic capitals in scripture and the prefaces use ordinary contextual case: Jesus, God, the Being, the Name, I am, and mixed-case inscriptions, including King of kings and Lord of lords. These are systematic decisions in `edition/revisions.json`, applied to quoted note lemmas too. Abbreviations and identifiers retain their uppercase spelling: TR, KJV, LXX, MS, MSS, BC, AD, initials, manuscript sigla and Roman numerals.
- Brenton's square brackets are not printed. In a book the Hebrew Bible has, they mark the Greek that the Hebrew lacks, as in Genesis 35:16, Proverbs 4:27a and the additions to Esther, and are dropped; the notes that explain what the Hebrew lacks stay, on those words, or name the verses where the passage runs over them. The two there that mark something else go too: at Exodus 5:14 they hold English supplied from the Hebrew, which prints as supplied words with its note, and at Judges 1:10 a parenthesis the Bagster printings added, which the 1844 printing does without. In a book it lacks, they mark English words with no Greek behind them, which the Authorized Version prints in italics in the books it has, and are marked as supplied words (`\add`) like the translators' others.
- Direct speech receives no added quotation marks. Existing quotation marks in prose are typographic; in the notes, italic takes their place around cited words.
- Greek is set in GFS Heraklit and Hebrew in Ezra SIL, in print and on the website, with Valkyrie; a build without Valkyrie sets the Greek in GFS Didot, with Utopia.

The README describes the page and its typeface, and [font normalization](font-normalization.md) explains how the typeface was assembled.
