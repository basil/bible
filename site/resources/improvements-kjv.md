## A Few Notes of Explanation

The main quotation of the Bible was taken from the King James Version. In the few places where the translation reads differently, it follows the New Scofield Version. The punctuation follows Mr. Pierpont's own style, simple and unencumbered.

This booklet was prepared with the hope that the readers would also apply the changes recommended to the other translations that are based on the same Greek text as the King James. These are the Old and New Scofield, George Ricker Berry (Greek Interlinear), the New King James, Young's Literal translation, and Jay Green's _Interlinear Bible_. The other translations available before 1611, the Webster Bible of 1825, and the King James II should also be similar to the King James. However, most of the other versions have tendencies toward the weaker Alexandrian Text as found in the Greek Texts of Westcott and Hort, the UBS, and Nestles. The corresponding English translations that have many minority readings are the NAS, NIV, Phillips, ASV, RSV, NEB, and almost every other modern translation.

Other considerations for changes beside the Majority Text are:

1. Translational: Be sure the version you compare with is using the same Greek words as the Majority Text.

2. Greek Tenses: The weakest area in the King James Version is in the precise translation of the Greek tenses---in which most modern versions are equally weak.

3. Word Origins: Age/Forever, Stake/Cross are only a few examples.

4. Literal vs. Paraphrase, Transliterations, Historical Present and Archaic words.

## Introduction

### Our English Bibles

God originally gave His Word to chosen men, whom He inspired for this purpose, to be written in their Hebrew and their Greek languages. From then on, these manuscripts (MSS) were carefully copied and recopied through the long centuries until the perfection of printing made mass reproduction possible. From these old MSS, compared together, men whom we call "textual critics" and editors prepared printed editions of the Hebrew and Greek "texts." It is from these printed "texts" that our English Bible versions have been translated.

In what follows we shall confine ourselves to the New Testament (NT).

### Why the Versions Differ

Many of us who love the Word of God and continue diligently to study it have come to the conclusion that the King James Version (KJV), or Authorized Version, is (in spite of its archaic English[^1]) preferable to most later versions. We shall see why this is a valid impression.

It does not seem to be widely recognized that the primary reason why most modern English versions of the NT differ significantly _from the KJV_ is because different underlying Greek "texts" have been used. Nor is it generally known that one of the major reasons why most modern versions differ so much _among themselves_ is because their underlying Greek "texts" derive from a relatively few ancient Greek MSS which frequently differ from each other. (Their translators follow sometimes one line and sometimes another.) The most prominent differences between these two types of Greek NT "texts" are in the _presence or absence_ of certain words, phrases and verses. Modern versions tend to _omit_ many things as compared to the KJV.

During the past century textual critics for the most part have mistakenly assumed that the ancient copyists were more likely to add something than to omit it. Therefore they "preferred the shorter reading." However, recent research into scribal habits has shown that the reverse is true: copyists were about three and a half times more likely to omit something than to add anything. This was their predominant error. Therefore we ought to be highly suspicious of omissions in one or a few old MSS.

Those who have examined the MSS have long known that very few MSS are exactly alike: almost every one differs in some ways from the rest. Usually it is an obvious slip in copying, but occasionally (particularly in antiquity) it is obvious that deliberate changes were made. At least from the time of St. Augustine the general rule for resolving these differences has been: The reading which the majority of MSS agree in is the correct reading. But most modern textual critics have abandoned this rule.

### The King James Version

The KJV was not a new translation, but a careful revision of existing English versions. It does not exactly agree with any known printed Greek NT "text," but comes closest to the Beza edition of 1598 (differing, it is said, from it in about 190 places). Sometimes it corresponds only loosely to any known Greek original, and in those places it follows exactly the Latin Vulgate version.

The men who edited (they were not really "translators") the KJV made no claim to have been Divinely inspired. Rather they specifically said that they did not consider their work to be final or perfect. They anticipated further improvements, both in knowledge of the underlying Greek "text" and in its understanding and translation.

Although very few Greek MSS were then known and used to prepare their Greek "texts," they were quite good representatives of the vast majority of MSS.[^2] Today, with many hundreds more of such MSS to guide us, a much more accurately representative "Majority Text" is available.[^3] Their good text is now improved. So, if we want to have the best possible NT, we ought to make those changes which the vast majority of MSS dictate.

### The Improvements Presented Here

The materials presented here are derived from the careful study of the textual notes in the following critical Greek NT editions: von Soden _Die Schriften des Neuen Testaments_, Tischendorf _Novum Testamentum Graece_ 8th edition, the Nestle and Nestle-Aland editions (together with the United Bible Societies 3rd edition). The underlying data was developed independently of the Hodges-Farstad edition, but carefully compared with it. (Other minor sources were also consulted.)

The changes noted here are not exhaustive, nor necessarily the best translations of these differences. Their aim is to pinpoint places where the representative Greek "text" produces a noticeable effect on the translation or meaning. Further exegetical study may bring out a few more such.

This listing does not include many instances where the KJV needs an improved translation to represent the (unchanged) Greek text properly.

### The Nature of the Majority Text, Often Called the "Byzantine Text"

The kind of Greek used in the Majority Text is exactly what would be expected from first century writers writing for the common man. It is not the terse, brief style of the old Greek classics which scholars liked and some tried to imitate. Instead it is like normal everyday speech and writing, full, simple, clear and unambiguous---just as the surviving letters and writings of that period illustrate. It was then this everyday style which some unbelieving Greek scholars criticized as the NT Scriptures began to circulate. That says something, doesn't it?

Textual critics who reject the Majority Textform as being representative of the autographs often refer to it as the "Byzantine Text." This implies that it is a "late" form of text and that it was localized in the Byzantium, or Constantinople areas, and would let them conveniently ignore it.

### The Manuscripts Which Underlie the New Testament

Most surviving MSS show evidence of careful---even meticulous---copying and of checking. In the papyri (oldest of all) we find "corrections," some of them from a Majority reading to something different, others from another reading to the Majority type. Accuracy of copying and meticulous checking are outstanding features of most MSS of all ages. (On the other hand, those few MSS which display deliberate editing also have the most careless errors. It almost seems that while they were ready to change, they were not interested in accuracy.) The vast majority of all MSS show a high degree of self-consistency, yet with no evidence of collusion or forced agreement.

In critical doctrinal areas the vast majority of MSS ring out loud and clear: the Person of Christ and the Resurrection, for example. These are what the writers personally witnessed. Yet in some of the few disagreeing MSS we find these areas are weakened or blurred, though not entirely absent. This is reflected in many modern versions, as a comparison will show.

There is a great abundance of evidence for the words of the NT, as this tabulation of MSS will show:

Some of these have been lost through fire, war, etc.; so that a grand total of NT MSS and Lectionaries is close to 5,000. Some of them are complete NT's, some individual books or collections of books; and quite a number are fragmentary. Some have just a few verses or parts of a verse. Nevertheless there are very many. There is an overabundance of evidence.

### Manuscripts Underlying the Present Data

Our basic sources reference from about 800 to 1200 MSS for the Gospels, approximately 290 MSS for the Epistles and Acts, and up to about 215 MSS for the Revelation (which includes almost every known available MS for it).

Modern textual critics have had little incentive to examine carefully the very many minuscule MSS. (Some are not available for study.) Their interest has been confined to MSS which differ from the Majority-Traditional Textform. Up to about 1000 have been examined to various degrees, and about half that number have had fairly careful study. Likewise, Lectionaries have been examined to some extent---there are very many of them---and their data is only partially available. (Lectionary evidence is not included in this material.) The uncial and papyri have been given the most examination, especially the earlier ones.

From this it can be seen that about one-third of the available material has been reported on. That this is quite adequate to certify the Majority Textform can be demonstrated by mathematical statistical theory. We may expect virtually no further change when and if all the MSS have been examined. Further notes may be found in the Appendix.

## How to Use These Notes

The _first_ column gives the chapter and verse.

The _second_ column contains the "weighting" number or letter of explanation. This classifies the changes as to their importance. The weighting values are:

- 5 or the letters _b_, _c_, _d_ means that the changed reading occurs in 95% or more of all the Byzantine, Majority manuscripts (at least 19 out of every 20). (for letters---see p. 6)

- 4 means that the changed reading is found in 80% to 94% of all these manuscripts.

These two groups of changes are therefore definitely in the majority, and _should be incorporated into our Versions_.

- 3 is still clearly in the majority, though not as strong. It represents 61% to 79% of all these manuscripts.

- 2 signifies a changed reading which may be expected to occur between 40% and 60% of the time---a truly alternate reading.

- 1 denotes a reading found in only 22% to 39% of these manuscripts. It is definitely in the minority and is given only for information and interest.

- \+ _or_ - These two symbols, located after some of the weighing numbers, indicate whether the support is more on the upper (+) or lower (-) portion of that level reading indicated.

The _third_[^4] column gives the reading of the King James Version where the change is to be found. Only enough of the context is given to identify clearly its location in the verse. Two marks are used to indicate the change: 1) the _underline_ denotes those words changed, and 2) the _plus sign_ (+) is used to show where added words are to be placed. The slash (/) indicates the end of the quoted portion. (The quotation often begins somewhere in the middle of the verse.) The beginning and end of the words quoted has no significance in itself.

The _right hand column_, or whatever follows the slash (/), gives the changes to be made. If the underlined word or words are to be omitted, this is indicated by omit. If the underlined words are to be replaced by other words, these words are found in this column. If words are to be added, they are preceded by the plus sign (+).

For example:

- _Deletion_ (omission): Woe _unto you_/ omit (Read: "Woe")

- _Change:_ give him as _many_ as he needs/ much (Read: "give him as much as he needs")

- _Addition:_ and + saith unto him/ + Jesus (Read: "and Jesus saith unto him")

In a very few places there will be more than one variation, but these are rare, and should be easily understood.

Several observations must be made regarding the use of these notes.

1. They are based solely upon the _Greek Majority Text_ form of the New Testament (Byzantine). They specifically do not include other Greek sources (Lectionaries, Greek Fathers), nor non-Greek sources (Versions and non-Greek Fathers).

2. They serve to alert the reader or translator to those significant places where the King James Version _differs_ from the Greek Majority Text form. They indicate the nature of the difference, rather than to prescribe a particular rendering.

   Only those places where the change is translatable, or potentially translatable, is there a note. The article is noted only when the Greek text itself has a difference in meaning which may be meaningful in English.

3. _Excluded_ from these notes are:

   1. words supplied in (_italics_) by the King James Translators which have no known basis in any text or version.

   2. those places where the King James Version _already agrees_ with the Majority Text, rather than the Received Text (where there is a real difference). (See page 36.)

   3. the many places where the King James Version _may be improved_ in its rendering of the Greek text (but the Text itself is not involved).

4. So, _the notes here then cover_ those differences to the King James Version which are or may be translatable, where:

   1. the King James version represents the Received Text, but the Majority Text reads differently from the Received Text. The notes having a _weighting number_ fall almost entirely under this category.

      There are three other situations, denoted by a _small letter_ instead of a weighting number. These changes are mandatory changes and correspond to a weighting of 5.

   2. It is evident that the King James Version must have used a Greek text other than the Received Text or the Majority Text (and Greek manuscript evidence can be found for it).

   3. The King James Version clearly deviates from any _Greek_ manuscript evidence (as verified by the critical editions), but it may have Latin Version support.

   4. The King James Version omits an item which all known Greek manuscript evidence appears to have.

The above comments apply to all except the Revelation, for which please see pages 25 and 35.

A half-parenthesis "(" precedes those notes which by their degree of support (weight 1) or by their nature (weight 2 omissions) are unlikely to be significant as possible alternatives---they are only informational and not of importance.

## Appendix

### Why the Majority Reading?

Any worthwhile textual work must have some guiding rules by which to establish the "best" readings---those most likely to have been original. Throughout human history it appears that the principle of accepting the testimony of the majority of agreeing witnesses has governed law and science. It is reasonable. (A majority of _witnesses_ is quite different from a majority _opinion_.) The evidence we have makes it safe to say that:

1. We have hardly two MSS today which are exactly identical (no collusion).

2. Faithful copying and checking are evidenced almost throughout the MSS.

3. All of the texts which the various MSS contain are very old, going back at least into the second century.

4. The Church Fathers (bishops, etc.) in early days knew of and protested against, destroying when they could, MSS which were clearly the work of heretical scribes and editors.

5. The oldest MSS (including, of course, the originals) should have the most "children," simply because they were earliest. We should expect, then, that the majority would best represent those originals, under normal conditions.

6. The Minuscules (written in small letters and more or less like handwriting rather than handprinting) are simply copies of earlier Uncials (those written in capital letters, like printing). (Earlier modern textual critics tried hard to insist that this difference in letter forms was further evidence of the "secondary" nature of the Traditional-Majority Textform. All that could be said was that they were later copies.)

7. The Church authorities and scholars of antiquity must have had occasion to recognize good copies from bad or poor ones (and they were closer to the beginnings). They would be expected to copy only those they deemed reliable. (Also expensive materials were involved, not to be wasted.)

If we grant the truth of these propositions, then:

1. The MSS we have available today must quite faithfully represent an almost equal number in existence in the second century or earlier, and if

2. the majority we have today represent the majority in circulation in that early period, then they should truly represent the text in its earliest form.

This is the basis for the principle that the Majority Reading is the "best."

### Copying---Scribal Habits

Ancient pagan scribes for a millennium or two before Christ came, went to school to learn how to copy accurately. They might pay with their lives for a slip in a government or sovereign's document copied! Ancient Hebrew scribes must have had similar careful training. They knew what mistakes were easiest to make and how to check and verify their work. Hebrew scribes were further motivated in that it was not just a human ruler's words they might be copying, but the Words of God Himself.

We do not know who copied the NT Scriptures in the earliest period. But from about the 4th or 5th century onward the professional scribe worked, mostly under Church supervision. In the Greek-speaking world there was no "standard" MS, but rather each MS was copied just as it was, regardless of any differences from other MSS they had available.

The fact that the Jewish Christian influenced the early church of the East and that the NT writings were recognized as Scripture, even in the first century, certainly suggests meticulous copying of those earliest MSS, at least those copies intended for other Christian churches where they would be trusted and used. So we may suspect that "professional" standards prevailed.

Alphonse Dain, a French MS expert of wide experience, wrote in his book _Les Manuscrits_ that the very commonest scribal error is omission---ranging from just simply leaving something out to omitting everything between two identical letter groups (homoeoteleuton). Surely every ancient scribe was well aware of this and went to great lengths to avoid it. Only unlearned or careless copyists would be expected to leave things out.

We also know that in first century Rome the demand for books was so great that scribes there rushed them out in quantity, copies so bad that their living authors protested vehemently.

Recent detail examinations of the over 200 MSS of the Revelation have shown that the vast majority of Scribes were most careful copyists, with very few indeed who dared edit or be careless. This study confirms Dain's conclusion.

The Eastern Church has historically resisted any alteration or editing of its MSS. They have insisted that each parent MS be copied exactly as it is. So scrupulously has this principle been observed that even where two MSS which they had differed somewhat from each other, and even when copied side by side, each maintained its own true character without intermixing, even when the same scribe copied both.

### Variations Within the Majority Text Manuscripts

Earlier modern textual critics assumed that the Traditional-Majority MSS, which they derisively termed "Byzantine," were rigidly almost identical. Factual study refuted this assumption. Later attempts to classify them into families have been only partly successful because these differences among them run like colored threads in a richly intertwined pattern throughout the various MSS. Most of them are of such a minor nature that they do not appear at the level of translation at all. This is in glaring contrast to the sort of differences found from and within the Alexandrian-Minority group of MSS.

### The "Received Text"---The "TR"

This term generally refers to one of the several different Greek NT's printed primarily before about 1850, though many have been printed subsequently. They differ in about 140 places from each other. Any one of them is a good basic text, differing mostly in small ways from the Majority consensus. The most significant differences are the ones given here.

### The Non-Majority Manuscripts and Texts

Textual critics first discerned two general groups of MSS: those which agreed together quite well---the Majority group---and those which differed significantly. Later they began to see the minority group as composed of two groups: the Alexandrian or Egyptian and the "Western" group. Finally, a fourth group has been claimed. The "Western" group, or "texttype," does not really exist in the sense that the Alexandrian texttype seems to. It is really a Latin thing. The so-called "Caesarean" texttype is discerned by some and denied by others. Probably the wiser course is simply to view these three minority groups as being variations of the dissenting MSS, with the "Caesarean" as a sort of hybrid approaching the Majority more closely than the others. Some critics have reverted to the original two groups: the majority (which they wholeheartedly reject as "secondary") and the minority (which they accept).

The popular _Alexandrian_ text form is noteworthy in two aspects of language: 1) its brevity (there are very many omissions of all kinds), and 2) the semi-classical type of Greek used. One especially noteworthy feature evident in the very oldest manuscripts is the extreme carelessness in copying. Any examination of the footnotes to the critical editions of this text will show the many and wide divergences of the various manuscripts. As one careful critic has said, "No amount of honest copying could ever produce such manuscripts as these."

Disagreement among themselves is a noteworthy characteristic of this minority group. Quite often, in fact, individual MSS or several of them together, agree with the Majority group where others differ. They are a motley crew.

The danger which lies in these often mutilated passages is that the truth is to that degree "unbalanced." When such passages are used, there is bound to be some distortion of God's truth. It is a risk we dare not take.

### Further notes on the Manuscripts Underlying the Present Data

Our most basic source, von Soden, analyzed about 1250 MSS in the _Gospels_. Of these 60 were of the Alexandrian group, and the rest belong to or are close to the Majority, which he termed "Koine" (common), although he called some of them "Jerusalem" group. For the _Acts and Epistles_ he listed about 350 on the average, of which he classed 15--24 as Alexandrian.

However, the reader must not jump to the conclusion that Soden actually used this total number for any particular reading. (Actually he had about forty "assistants" scattered over Europe gathering and compiling data for him.) He did not detail for us the methods he and his assistants used. He had no direct interest in the Majority Textform except as it influenced his arbitrary "two out of three" rule for what he would put into his text. He did, in spite of this general disinterest, provide us with a great deal of
useful information. Sometimes he does give some numbers, particularly where a major group of MSS is divided in the support of a reading. Yet even these may not tell us the full facts as to support. There is good reason, however, to believe that his statement that "this is a Majority Text reading" is sound. (He did this symbolically, of course.)

For the _Revelation_, Hoskier's large volumes supply almost exhaustively what can be known. Although he reported on about 215 MSS, only about 158 to 179 exist for any particular verse. (This is because of damage through the years to the MSS.) Here we find the majority of MSS divided into two large groups: the Andreas group (A-group, or An group) of about 80 MSS; and the other group denoted by Q, of about 75 MSS. This is not a sharp division, but with a good many MSS falling at various places in between. There are about 210 places where they differ significantly (in the Greek, though not necessarily so in English). There are also a number of smaller groups, having varying loyalties.

### Let's Keep Things in Perspective

It is easy to exaggerate the situation because it is God's own Word. There are about 140,000 Greek words in the whole NT. The worst MS befuddles about 1/8 of them---that's a lot! But the vast majority agree much better, infinitely better, for less than one-half of one percent are open to any question and most of these have no effect on translation or meaning. We have an excellent text in the Majority Greek NT, a trustworthy volume of Scripture.

## Is Your Bible Missing Verses?

(Most modern Bibles are!)

With the discovery of many ancient Greek Bibles during the 1800s, it became increasingly obvious that the King James Greek text of the New Testament was in need of some changes, because most of these manuscripts differed occasionally from it.

The initiative for change, however, came from those in opposition to the King James text, rather than from a concern for accuracy in its supporters. The resulting text from those opposed to the King James Version introduced the procedure of changing and removing words, phrases, verses, and even sections of the New Testament on the basis of only a few or even one Greek manuscript. Some of the necessary corrections were made, but then many times more errors were introduced by this means. Most modern Bible translators have adopted most of these errors and even added to them by choosing readings from a handful of Greek manuscripts that have many independent readings and show many signs of carelessness and deliberate editing.

These needless errors would be eliminated by adopting the reading of the _overwhelming_ majority of the Greek manuscripts. Since each of the Greek Bibles was manually copied from another Bible there are occasional errors. However, well over three-fourths of the Greek manuscripts have the characteristics of being reliably and faithfully copied.

Though the King James New Testament is characterized by faithfulness to reliable manuscripts, in a few places it has borrowed from the Latin Vulgate. The Latin Vulgate is the Bible used and altered by the Roman Catholic church since the fourth century.
